Not every question deserves the same six pages

Today every question gets one treatment: find the six best pages, hand them to the AI, get an answer. That is right for half of what gets asked and wrong for the rest. This is the map of question kinds, how each one is best answered, and what we build.

The question kinds

Nine kinds cover everything the logs show. Three already have their own path; six are about to get one.

KindExampleThe right way to answerStatus
Instant "What's today's date?" · "Who's waiting on me?" No AI at all — read the number straight from the data. 0 ms. built
Action "Archive Meridian" · "Add these prospects" Not a question. Route to the tools, do it, confirm in one line. built today
Lookup "Who's the decision maker at Northwind?" The current pipeline: six best pages, answer, cite. Fast and right. built
Timeline "What happened with Falcon since June?" Six pages can't hold three months. Pull wide (12–18 pages), order them by date, and ask for the story in time order. building
Count / sweep "How many deals mention pricing?" · "List all July meetings" "Best six" is the wrong idea entirely — counting needs everything, not the best. Sweep the structured data (ledger, panels, calendar, date index) and enumerate before writing prose. building
Multi-hop "Who introduced me to Sam?" · "Which of Morgan's colleagues saw the demo?" Two questions in a chain: answer the first hop, then search again with what you learned. The brain's people-and-company links are the map; the agent is told to walk them step by step. building
Absence "Did we ever invoice Northwind?" · "Anything I promised and not delivered?" The dangerous kind — a thin search plus confidence invents facts. Search wide, then say what was searched before saying "no record", so an honest no is distinguishable from a lazy one. building
Compare / judge "Which deal should I chase first?" · "Is this price in line with past deals?" Gather facts per candidate first — one mini-lookup each — then reason across them. Judging from whatever six pages happened to surface skews toward whoever emails the most. building
Multi-part "When is the talk due, and who has the draft?" Split it. Answer every part, and check that every part got answered — the second clause is what silently drops today. building

The harness change — a router, then playbooks

What we take from oh-my-pi's supervisor idea, adapted: the "supervisor" here is cheap code, not another AI. String rules sort the question in under a millisecond (the same trick that fixed question-vs-action this morning); each kind then gets its own playbook — how wide to search, in what order, and a short set of marching orders in the prompt. The expensive part, reasoning across steps, stays inside the one agent, which already knows how to use the search and graph tools when told to.

Why not a real multi-agent setup first? A supervisor model that plans, spawns worker agents, and judges their answers would add 10–30 seconds and a second bill to every question, including the easy majority. The median answer is 11 seconds today and that is worth protecting. The plan: routing + playbooks first, measure, and reach for true multi-turn decomposition only where the benchmark says playbooks aren't enough. The harness is built so that step is an addition, not a rewrite.

Multi-turn, where it earns its place

Two places already justify a second turn, and both are bounded:

Ambiguity. "What about the pilot?" with three pilots in flight should cost you one short counter-question, not a guess. The agent is allowed one clarifying question when the target is genuinely ambiguous — the same rule the drafting tool has used from day one.

Self-check on the risky kinds. For absence and multi-part answers, the playbook ends with a check: "before replying — did you answer every part? did you name what you searched?" One instruction, not a second agent; the eval loop will tell us if it needs teeth.

How we know it worked

The benchmark grows a category per kind — count, multi-hop, compare, multi-part — alongside the existing recall, temporal and no-data traps. Baseline runs before the change ship with it; the nightly loop then owns the numbers: a kind that regresses files itself as work, and the answers-too-simply complaint becomes a graded score instead of a feeling.
